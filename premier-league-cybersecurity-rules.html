The Premier League's New Cybersecurity Rules, Explained

166 requirements. Three deadlines. Reports to the League every January and every April. Here is what the rulebook says and who at a club has to own it.

EDJ Consulting · 30 October 2026

Download the one-page summary (PDF), 1 page, opens in a new tab

On 31 July 2026 the Premier League published its Handbook for the 2026/27 season. It runs to more than 900 pages. On page 693 is a section that most people have not read: Appendix 11, the Premier League Information Security Baselines.

It is the first time the League has made cybersecurity a formal rule. Until this season, clubs had a security baseline described in 2024 as "non-prescriptive." Now they have requirements, deadlines, and an obligation to prove compliance to the League with evidence. The clubs approved the change at the League's annual meeting in June 2026, after two seasons of consultation.

This article explains what the rules require, when, and what it will take to meet them. It is based on the published Handbook. The page numbers are given so you can check.

What the rules require

The requirements are in Appendix 11 (pages 693 to 713). The obligations are in Rules J.9 to J.14 (pages 172 to 174).

There are 166 requirements in 22 areas. The areas cover the full range of a modern security programme: risk management, governance, policies, personnel security, access and authentication, asset management, network security, endpoint hardening, anti-malware, email security, patching, data retention, data classification, Internet of Things devices, backups, assurance, incident response, cyber insurance, third-party risk, accreditation, protective monitoring, and audit.

The requirements come into force in three phases:

Phase Requirements Deadline
Phase One 48 30 April 2027
Phase Two 29 30 April 2028
Phase Three 89 30 April 2029

Once a phase deadline passes, the club must keep meeting those requirements permanently. Rule J.9 says "on a continuing basis thereafter."

What is due first

The deadlines above are only half the picture. Rules J.10 to J.12 set up a reporting cycle that runs every season, with no end date.

  1. By 10 January, each club must send the League an interim assessment of its compliance with the requirements that apply to it.
  2. Within 28 days, if the club is not fully compliant, it must send the League a detailed plan setting out how it will get there.
  3. By 30 April, each club must send the League a final assessment with supporting evidence demonstrating compliance.
  4. At any time, the League can require a club to provide additional information and evidence (Rule J.11).

The first interim assessment is due on 10 January 2027. That is the first date on which a club finds out whether its current arrangements can produce a report the League will accept.

The January report is a self-assessment. It is not the hard part. The hard part is April, because April requires evidence. It is easy to say "we have multi-factor authentication." It is a different job to prove it, for every one of 48 requirements, in a form the League accepts, and then do it again next year for 77 requirements, and the year after that for 166.

What happens if a club falls short

The cybersecurity section has no penalties of its own. A breach is handled under the League's general disciplinary rules. The League Board can issue a reprimand, impose a fine of up to £100,000 through its summary jurisdiction, or refer the case to an independent commission.

For a Premier League club, a £100,000 fine is not the real cost. The real cost is a board being asked by the League for evidence it does not have, and discovering that nobody inside the club owns the answer.

There is also an exception route. Under Rule J.13, the League can grant a dispensation from specific requirements where a club shows it cannot comply because of exceptional circumstances. The League can attach conditions.

The two rules that require outside help

Two parts of Appendix 11 are worth reading closely, because they write external assurance into the rulebook.

Control 22.b is in Phase One. It says: "An audit plan is agreed upon with a third party auditor for independent security assessments each season." Every club must engage an outside auditor for an independent security assessment, every season, starting with the first phase. This is not a later-phase ambition. It is due by April 2027.

Controls 20.a to 20.c are in Phase Three. By April 2029, every club must select and implement a recognised security framework (20.a), have executive management review and approve it (20.b), and undergo regular third-party audits against it (20.c). In practice this means choosing a standard such as ISO 27001, the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, or the NCSC Cyber Assessment Framework, getting board sign-off, and being audited against it by an outside firm.

Phase One looks technical. Phase Three is not.

Read the 48 Phase One requirements and a pattern appears. Most of them are technical controls: endpoint detection and response on every device, multi-factor authentication, firewalls at every network boundary, network segmentation, email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC), email filtering, immutable backups, a documented incident response plan. An IT provider can install most of these. But installation is not what the League is assessing. Each of the 48 has to be self-assessed by 10 January 2027, covered by a remediation plan within 28 days if it falls short, and evidenced to the League by 30 April. That is a programme with a deadline, and at most clubs nobody has been handed it.

Now read the 89 Phase Three requirements. They are different in kind. The club must document its risk appetite. It must have a methodology for measuring whether its security controls work. It must manage third-party risk with KPIs. It must collect security logs in one place, make them tamper-proof, establish a baseline of normal activity, and test its detection capability every year. It must maintain a three-year information security strategy. Ten of the thirteen protective monitoring requirements are in Phase Three. Seven of the nine third-party risk requirements are in Phase Three.

None of that is IT work. It is programme management. An IT provider does not own it, because it is not what an IT provider is for. At most clubs, nobody owns it yet. More than half the rulebook sits in that gap, and the first half is already on the clock.

The club that was one click away

The cost of a security gap in football is not theoretical. In January 2017 a senior official at Manchester City received an email that appeared to come from UEFA. It contained a link to a financial compliance report. The official clicked download. The email was a phishing attack by a hacker by the name Rui Pinto. City's servers were breached and thousands of internal emails and documents were taken. They became the Football Leaks material published by Der Spiegel in 2018, and the foundation of the Premier League's case against the club. In September 2026 the club was reported to have been found in breach of 114 of 115 charges.

Whatever the final outcome of that case, it started with one email and one click. Four of the 48 Phase One requirements address exactly that: email authentication to block spoofed senders (10.a), email filtering (10.c), phishing awareness training for staff (10.e), and compensating controls for when a phishing email gets through anyway (10.f).

Promoted clubs have the least time

Rule J.14 covers clubs promoted into the League. A promoted club must meet every phase whose deadline has already passed, by the next deadline. The Handbook gives its own example: a club promoted for the 2027/28 season must meet Phase One and Phase Two by 30 April 2028.

That means a Championship club with ambitions for promotion is already on the clock. The club with the smallest back office and the least warning will have the most to do in the shortest time.

What this looks like from the other side of the Atlantic

The model the Premier League has adopted is not new. It is the model United States defence contractors have worked under for about a decade. A government body publishes a list of required security controls. Companies self-assess against it. Where they fall short, they file a plan of action with dates. On a fixed cycle, they produce evidence that an assessor will accept. The names are different (NIST 800-171, CMMC, a "POA&M" instead of a "detailed plan"), but the structure is the same, down to the 28-day remediation plan.

That market made three mistakes in its first years, and clubs can avoid all of them.

First, treating the evidence package as an IT task. Companies handed the requirements to their IT provider and assumed compliance would follow. It did not, because half the requirements were about governance, risk, and documentation, and the IT provider was not hired to do those. The programme needs an owner who sits above the vendors, not inside one of them.

Second, letting the IT provider grade its own work. A self-assessment completed by the same people who run the systems tends to come back green. Assessors learned to distrust it. Clubs should expect the League, and the third-party auditor required by 22.b, to do the same.

Third, confusing "implemented" with "documented." Many companies had the controls in place and still failed, because they could not prove it. A control that exists but cannot be evidenced does not count. The organisations that passed cleanly were the ones that built a programme: a calendar of what gets checked and when, a defined evidence artifact for every requirement, and a single binder (physical or digital) that could be handed to an auditor on request.

That last point is the whole job. Compliance with Appendix 11 is not 166 technical tasks. It is a programme that produces 166 pieces of evidence, every year, on a schedule the League has already set.

Who owns this at your club?

The practical question for every club is not whether the IT provider can install the Phase One controls. It is who owns the programme: the yearly calendar, the January assessment, the 28-day plan if needed, the April evidence package, the relationship with the auditor, and the report to the board.

If the answer is a named person with the time and the experience to do it, the club is in good shape. If the answer is a pause, the first interim assessment is due on 10 January.
